Why awaken to our fundamental oneness, if all it means is we’re alone, at the end of the day, when novelty has finally run dry? When the universe is over, when forgetting is over. We return, brain-storm, invent new ways to forget. This is if God is a guy in his room all bored to tears, nothing to do but dream. With the world as merely a scenic backdrop, and everything real and alive and lovable coming from within the human world, we can only imagine the ultimate reality as our selves or tools or culture taken to its logical conclusion. Nobody believes in a consumerist chocolate egg-gobbling God, but plenty of highly disembodied boys give a high credence to the possibility of a computer programmer God—even though in the conceptual space of imaginable cosmologies these are neighbours, neither more or less plausible than the other.

Reality, someone said, is not only stranger than we imagine—it is stranger than we *can* imagine.

Of course the clock was the big metaphor for a while, and here’s what a bunch of guys were saying: (summary from chatgpt)

1. Isaac Newton: Newton's mechanistic worldview, which played a critical role in the scientific revolution, was often likened to a clock. The universe was viewed as a vast, deterministic machine, set in motion by God and then left to run according to established laws of motion. This concept of "clockwork universe" was influential in the development of modern science.
2. René Descartes: Descartes, a contemporary of Newton, also embraced a clockwork vision of the universe. His metaphor of the world as a machine (with living beings as smaller machines within it) had a profound impact on the development of mechanical philosophy and later, modern science.
3. Gottfried Leibniz: Responding to Newton, Leibniz critiqued the idea of a clockwork universe that ran without divine intervention. Leibniz proposed that God not only created the universe-machine but also continually maintains and renews it.
4. Karl Marx: Marx used the clock as a symbol of industrial capitalism. In his view, the regimentation of time in factories, where workers' lives were governed by the factory clock, was dehumanizing and alienating.
5. Henri Bergson: Bergson critiqued the scientific view of time as uniform and measurable, likening it to the ticking of a clock. In contrast, he proposed the idea of "duration" (la durée) - an indivisible, immeasurable, subjective experience of time.
6. Martin Heidegger: Heidegger wrote about the importance of "authentic time." For him, the uniform, clock-time of modern industrial societies is inauthentic because it obscures our being-towards-death - the anticipation of our own mortality that gives our lives meaning.
7. Albert Einstein: With the theory of relativity, Einstein dramatically altered our understanding of time, showing that time is not absolute and constant (like the ticking of a clock), but relative to the observer's speed and gravitational field.

A life cycle of sorts starts to come into view for these things. The clock metaphor once extended to the whole universe and all life, but now it doesn’t even cut the mustard for time. In between many smart guys who considered themselves residents of the cutting edge believed it literally true.
