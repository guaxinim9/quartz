> 1. Cognitive Dissonance (the classic)
> This is the basic idea that when people hold two contradictory beliefs (e.g., “Israel is good” and “Israel is bombing civilians”), they’ll often twist the reality to preserve the belief they’re more emotionally attached to. So the more Israel behaves monstrously, the more fervently someone might double down on their support—because admitting the truth would cause too much internal conflict.
> “Sure, it looks bad... but they’re defending themselves.”
> 2. Belief Perseverance
> This refers to the tendency to maintain a belief even after the original evidence for it has been discredited. Once the belief is part of your identity (your worldview, your tribe, your ego), letting go of it feels like death.
> 3. The Backfire Effect
> When confronted with contrary evidence, people often dig in *deeper* to their existing beliefs. It's a kind of egoic self-defense mechanism. Reality knocks, and the soul bolts the door.
> 4. Group Identity and Social Signaling
> For your father or others, support for Israel may not be primarily about geopolitics. It may be a symbolic allegiance to the broader Western project, Judeo-Christian values, or anti-antisemitism. Changing his view might feel like betraying his whole moral compass—or his tribe.
